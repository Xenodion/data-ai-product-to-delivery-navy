## Session 1 — the six questions

| 1 | **EU AI Act:** which risk tier is our agent in, and why? (minimal · limited — transparency: tell the user it is an AI · high-risk · prohibited) | Minimal to limited risk. It is an internal copilot used by the fintech's own support agents; a human validates every ticket and sends every reply, so the merchant never talks to the AI. It does not score creditworthiness, decide on onboarding or execute payments, which are the fintech-related uses that would push it towards high-risk. | 2 | **Data:** whose data does it use, and on what legal basis? (GDPR art. 6; what the B2B contract allows) | Merchants' account data and tickets, and personal data inside transactions (payers' names, IBAN fragments, merchant staff contacts). Likely basis: performance of the contract (art. 6(1)(b)) for support, or legitimate interest (art. 6(1)(f)); the exact role of the fintech (controller or processor) is not settled. Rule from day one: lookups are scoped to the merchant of the ticket, so merchant A's data can never reach merchant B's draft. The fine-tuned model is trained on synthetic tickets only. Our own prototype uses synthetic data only. | DPO; the B2B contract clauses; the LLM provider's data-processing terms (region, no training on our data) |
| 3 | **Accountability:** who is accountable when it is wrong — and where is the human? | The support agent who sends the reply remains accountable for what leaves the company; the Head of Support owns the quality of the support outcome; our team, as product owner, owns the copilot's behaviour and its rollback. The human sits between the draft and the merchant on every ticket (L2). An agent can ignore the draft or switch the copilot off for a ticket; a company-wide switch back to "reading only" (no draft) or off is to be designed in S4. | Head of Support (named owner to confirm); S4 for the override switch |
| 4 | **Never:** what must it never do? (our first guardrail) | First guardrail: **never send anything to a merchant** — it drafts, a human sends. Further guardrails to confirm: never state a payment status, amount or date that is not backed by a linked transaction; never guess a field it cannot determine (it returns `UNDEFINED`, and `UNDEFINED` always goes to a support agent); never promise a refund or a dispute outcome; never give legal or regulatory advice (it flags, the agent or Compliance decides); never use another merchant's data. | The team; Compliance |
| 5 | **Explainability:** explainable to whom — the customer, the support agent, the auditor? | *Support agent:* can open any ticket and see what the copilot read (the JSON fields) and which transaction or past ticket each claim in the draft comes from; fields it could not determine show as `UNDEFINED`. *Merchant:* receives an ordinary human reply that names the transactions concerned; they can ask the agent why. *Auditor:* a log per ticket — retrieved items, draft, final text sent, edits, who sent it, model version, the decision path taken and the draft. Retention period of that log is not decided. | DPO; Compliance (retention) |
| 6 | **The people it cannot serve:** what happens to them? | Tickets where evidence is thin (new merchants with little history, rare dispute types, English-language or forwarded-thread tickets we did not test) come back with `UNDEFINED` fields: a support agent works them as today, but they could end up slower than assisted tickets. We will report handling time and QA for `UNDEFINED` tickets and by merchant segment (size, language, age of account) as a counter-metric, so that a gain on the average cannot hide a loss there. We also report by agent seniority: published results on AI assistance for support agents (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, arXiv:2304.11771) find the gain concentrated among less experienced agents, with small quality declines for the most experienced. The agents are users too: no forced use, and no target that blames them for the tool. | S5 (segments and counter-metrics); Head of Support |

## Session 2 — the cost of error
> **Stale:** the text below was written for the superseded case (dispute drafts excluded, per-error euro figures from the old sizing). Redo it after the S2 sizing is rebuilt on the frozen brief; keep the structure.
- Which cost-of-error line is a **legal or reputational** risk, not just money? **A wrong status, amount or deadline stated to a merchant on a dispute or a PSD2 / KYC question** (Inputs row 31 in `02_sizing.xlsx`, ≈ €250 per error on money alone, analogy: Mastercard 2025, $128 per chargeback). The merchant acts on it (misses a chargeback deadline, re-ships, waits) and can complain to the regulator; payment-service providers have information and complaint-handling duties under PSD2. As in Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149), the company answers for what its tools tell customers, even though a human sends the reply here. This is why dispute tickets are **excluded from drafting in year 1** (context only), and why the copilot never states a status, amount or date that is not backed by a linked transaction (S1, question 4). Payout errors (≈ €120 each, guess) are mainly money: extra contacts, goodwill credits, a churn tail.
- Who in the company **owns** that risk? **The Head of Compliance** owns the legal exposure: the PSD2 / KYC wording the copilot may flag, and the decision to bring disputes back into scope. **The Head of Support** owns the day-to-day quality of replies and the switch back to "context only" (S1, question 3). Our team, as product owner, owns the measurement: the uncaught-wrong-reply rate (bar: ≤ 0.5 % of drafts) reported on every eval run from S4.
